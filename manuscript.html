Employment interview · 8–10 minutes

PurePulse interview manuscript

A walkthrough of recruiting, workforce operations and marketing. Each section includes a screen cue, suggested narration and speaker notes.

Live public and authenticated screens were inspected on November 25, 2026. Screenshots use public views, blank forms and cropped administrative controls. Verify your individual contribution before presenting.

1. PurePulse as an interview case study

On screen. Show the homepage and point to the consultation and portal entry points.

Say. I’m using PurePulse to show how I approach a workflow from both sides. The person outside the business needs a clear next step, and the person inside needs a way to pick up the work. I’ll show the public experience, then the administrative controls, and close with how I’d improve the process.

Speaker notes. Before presenting, prepare one concrete example of what you personally built or owned, the decision you made, and what you learned.

2. Affiliate recruitment and asynchronous pre-screening

On screen. Show the briefing and response controls, then the authenticated interview review entry point.

Say. The affiliate application introduces the opportunity with a video briefing. Applicants can record answers asynchronously in the browser or type them. The displayed question asks for at least 30 seconds of video or 300 typed characters. The page shows agreement and portal activation as subsequent steps. On the admin side, Candidate Video Interviews exposes asynchronous review, a scorecard workflow and candidate links. I’d use consistent, role-relevant prompts and have a reviewer document the next step.

Speaker notes. The public interface shows four required questions in some places and nine in others. The briefing badge says two minutes while the player shows 7:29. Mention these as concrete QA findings if asked. Recording, submission, agreement signing, activation and scoring were not exercised. Keep affiliate applications and employee hiring decisions distinct.

3. Employee onboarding and role-based accounts

On screen. Show the blank invitation form. Point to Role and Send Invite.

Say. The Team screen lets an administrator open an invitation and choose Admin, Manager, Member, or Intern. The form says it sends an email invitation. For a team of 10 to 99 employees, I’d use a standard onboarding checklist covering who approves access, what role the person needs, which work they own, and who confirms that they’re ready for day one. I’d verify the employee’s own account view and make offboarding part of the same process.

Speaker notes. Use 10–99 as a design scenario. Capacity, permission boundaries, invite delivery, activation and employee self-service were not tested. A useful example to rehearse is why a person doing regular project work would start with Member access.

4. Contractors and year-end reporting

On screen. Show the 1099 Documents entry point and blank Generate 1099 form.

Say. The admin interface has a 1099 document area with a client selector and a tax-year field. I’d confirm how that record maps to the actual payee before relying on it. In the operating process, finance would reconcile approved contractor payments to the ledger, resolve missing or duplicate records, and confirm which documents need to be prepared. I’d route employee year-end reporting through payroll and have finance own review and filing.

Speaker notes. The UI uses the label Client. Generation, exports, filing, employee tax forms and year-end reconciliation remain unverified. For the interview, describe the handoff and ownership without quoting the page’s tax guidance.

5. Time clocks and exception handling

On screen. Point to the client selector, work description and Clock In control.

Say. The Time Clock ties work to a client and a description. The interface also exposes manual entry, weekly progress, and recent entries. For a 10–99 employee workflow, I’d give employees a way to flag missed punches, have managers review corrections, and verify what payroll can consume. Billable project time and payable employee time need their own rules and checks.

Speaker notes. Clock-in, clock-out, approval history and payroll integration were not tested. For a dry run, use a fictional missed punch and walk through who submits, reviews and approves the correction.

6. Email marketing and campaign review

On screen. Show Marketing Broadcasts and the separate Campaigns workspace.

Say. Marketing Broadcasts exposes client and affiliate templates along with edit, preview and test-send controls. A separate Campaigns workspace describes reviewing generated content for client campaigns. I’d specify one audience, one purpose and one action for each email, then have an owner approve a test before sending. Afterward, I’d review delivery, clicks, replies and opt-outs to decide what to change.

Speaker notes. The Campaigns workspace showed an empty state during inspection. No messages were sent and no delivery or analytics were verified.

7. Social calendar and scheduling pilot

On screen. Show the live calendar header and Compose Post scheduling field, then walk through the proposed calendar.

Say. Content Calendar and Social Publishing are already visible in the admin interface. Compose Post has a platform selector and a scheduling field, and the workspace displays queue, scheduled and published states. My proposal is to pilot a weekly rhythm around those controls. Assign an owner and reviewer, schedule only approved work, and check the next business day that it actually posted.

8. Close and invite discussion

On screen. Return to the public and authenticated examples as needed.

Say. My approach is to get the workflow clear, build or verify the controls people need, and test the point where responsibility changes hands. Here, that means from applicant to reviewer, new hire to manager, time entry to payroll, and approved content to the publishing queue. I’d be happy to walk through a technical decision from the project or show how I’d test one of those handoffs.

Speaker notes. Rehearse a 30-second answer on your ownership, one technical tradeoff, and one result you can substantiate.
